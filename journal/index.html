The image below is a watercolor artwork I plan to use for my Every Picture studio project. The image itself, while having an interesting composition, is relatively simple to understand. It shows a woman's face with colorful hair being washed around in what seems to be water. However, the true complexity of it lies in relation to the story I plan to tell.

Currently, the narrative I'd like to build my project around is an exhibition showcase of the various artworks I've made, and how they reflect where I was characteristically at that point in my life. This particular artwork will come last, as it caps off my artistic journey and plays into the concept of cyclical nature.

My exhibition will start with one of my older artworks: a colorful, cartoonish medley of characters, visually vivid and playful. Then, I explore other pieces that stray away from colors, leaning more into realism, until I eventually come back to using color while attempting a challenging composition that I'm not comfortable with. This frames my narrative to represent a journey of transformation and return, as I develop new styles throughout my life (such as straying away from artistic mediums to stem), but ultimately return to the expressive nature that I began with (now pursuing design). I hope to tie this journey to how I changed personally throughout my life with more exploration and intensity.

Visual Thinking Strategies Research

February 9, 2026

As someone who finds enjoyment in theoretically analyzing narratives, imagery, and designed experiences, the concept of interactive images interests me. Although UI/UX design is my academic passion, I've spent a large chunk of my free time analyzing films, dissecting shots, and extracting the layered meanings that directors intentionally bury. As I read through “10 Intriguing Photographs to Teach Close Reading and Visual Thinking Skills” by Michael Gonchar, I realized that unique photographs achieve the same effect: a visual medium that when carefully designed, engages with the viewer.

I believe the article's core idea of “close reading” is very important in realizing the true nature and details of a narrative being presented to the viewer, and I found the metaphor of it being compared to reading in between the lines as clever. The various examples and guiding questions were helpful to exemplify how images can be differently interpreted, and they got me thinking of how imagery can be used to enhance the user experience of designed websites.

An interesting website I found that primarily uses images to engage with users is Ray-Ban's promotional page for their .EXE campaign. The site is primarily made to market a small selection of their products, and uses heavily stylized imagery and animations to achieve visual intrigue. I found the overall design language very thematic and effective for a site like this, where the main purpose is to draw in attention so viewers click to learn more about the products. Furthermore, the images are well designed to convey the cyberpunk aesthetic and create a narrative within the genre, while placing the actual products as the focal point and tying them into the story.

Overlays Design Pattern Research

January 29, 2026

Despite practicing UI/UX design, I have yet to extensively dabble with overlays when developing my sites, specifically for desktop views. Therefore, the article “Overuse of Overlays: How to Avoid Misusing Lightboxes” by Kathryn Whitenton turned out to be an interesting read that went into depth on understanding the effects of overlays on user experience, and the importance of overlay best practices if designers choose to implement them.

The article outlines the five W's (who, what, when, where, why) when analyzing overlays. Of these, I found the author's discussion of “what” to be fairly relatable. Their tips to create an explicit “cancel” option and a function for form overlays to save their inputs if accidentally closed, are design choices that sound obvious, but aren't always practiced. I've had experiences before where overlays are carefully designed to be difficult to exit out of, which makes me want to interact with them even less. Form overlays that don't save their inputs if closed also effectively pull me out of the experience as having to type out the same answers twice is tedious.

I also empathize with their observation of “when,” specifically their conclusion on the implementation of overlays that are not user initiated. I found that unexpected popups typically don't hold valuable information, and are used as cheap tricks for engagement bait, or to promote something that the user most likely wouldn't be interested in discovering if not forced to. This is commonly seen with advertisements, and hinders the user experience.

Research Form Design

January 20, 2026

Form design is something I haven't actively thought about when working on my previous UI/UX projects as I haven't had to integrate it as a feature, but it is something I've commonly interacted with on other websites. After reflecting on “Best practices for form design” by Salim Ansari, I've begun to understand the core principles that are accepted as effective design choices for engaging form user experience, with an emphasis on the following concepts: simplicity, transparency, accessibility, and microinteractions.

Simplicity is brought up many times, critical for not overwhelming the user with information. The idea of “less is more” rings true for effective form design, as seen in common techniques like minimal input fields, single column layouts, simplified wording, hidden drop down menus, and more.

Transparency is crucial for users to understand the details of the form, and to not get confused with what is being asked of them. This can be achieved through elements like separating required and optional fields (to clearly indicate what is necessary for the form), bullets or info symbols to explain intricate formatting, labels, placeholder text, and more.

Accessibility is a staple in any form of UI/UX design, and forms are no different. An interesting concept that reflects this is a tab-ready feature, which is where users are able to navigate to the next input field utilizing the tab button. Accessibility also refers to visual clarity, readability, responsiveness, and more.

Finally, microinteractions are just as important in form design as they are in all of UI/UX. Common features of microinteractions include feedback messages for errors or completion, stylized action buttons, and auto-focus first input field (field indication for the starting point of the form).

Original Article: "Research Form Design"

The ultimate goal of form design is to be efficient while effective in completing the required task. Spotify's login prompt is a solid example of good form design principles, as it utilizes its design system to create visual contrast for accessibility (font and color), clear action buttons to indicate how to interact with the form, and input prompts to communicate what users need to fill out.
